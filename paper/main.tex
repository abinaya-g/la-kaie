\title{Does the learning matter? A pre-registered deconstruction of SARSA-guided information exchange in the MPHBS
hybrid metaheuristic}

\author[1]{Abinaya G\corref{cor1}}
\ead{gabinaya@saveetha.ac.in}
\cortext[cor1]{Corresponding author.}
\affiliation[1]{organization={Department of Information Technology, Saveetha Engineering College},
                city={Chennai}, country={India}}
% PENDING: co-authors (e.g. supervisor) and their affiliations

\begin{abstract}
MPHBS couples the Honey Badger Algorithm and the Marine Predators Algorithm through a mediator that assembles hybrid
solutions dimension by dimension under the control of a SARSA-style value table, and its original evaluation attributes
part of its performance to this learning, mainly through a comparison with a ``matched'' random mirror. We show
analytically and from the published code that this mirror is not matched per iteration: it spends $N$ additional
evaluations in every iteration, so it runs 21\% (CEC2022) and 16\% (FIR) fewer iterations at the same budget, and under
the CEC2022 configuration it also refreshes a population memory that MPHBS leaves unchanged after the mediator. We then
report a pre-registered study with budget-identical controls on the CEC2022 suite ($D=20$) and eight FIR filter-design
problems ($D=31$): \res{runs-total} seed-paired runs executed once and analysed with a frozen script. A random mediator at
the same budget performs at least as well as SARSA on both benchmarks (mean paired win probability of SARSA
\res{H1-CEC2022-wbar}, 95\% CI \res{H1-CEC2022-ci}, and \res{H1-FIR-wbar}, \res{H1-FIR-ci}). What matters is the
exchange step itself, which halves the median error on FIR, and how the population memory is handled: refreshing it, as
the published mirror does, is strongly harmful on CEC2022 and, in a post hoc diagnostic, collapses population diversity. The restructured main loop
of MPHBS is consistently but negligibly better than the original backbone. An exploratory extension to the 24 BBOB functions
replicates the absence of a learning effect and shows that the exchange step helps on separable but hurts on rotated,
non-separable functions. We formalise budget- and state-matched
ablation controls and give a protocol for constructing them.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Learned control components have become a common ingredient of population-based metaheuristics. Reinforcement learning
selects variation operators, adapts parameters during the run, or decides how solution information flows between
cooperating populations~\cite{karafotias2015parameter,drugan2019rl}. A learned controller is usually justified with an
ablation that replaces it by a random or fixed one: if the learned variant performs better, the difference is attributed
to learning. This inference is valid only if the two variants differ in nothing but the learning. When the controlled
variant also changes how many objective function evaluations (FEs) each iteration consumes, how many iterations fit into
the budget, or how the populations' internal state evolves, the comparison attributes to learning an effect that has a
different cause.

This paper examines one recent instance of the problem in detail. MPHBS~\cite{flores2026mphbs} hybridises the Honey
Badger Algorithm (HBA)~\cite{hashim2022hba} and the Marine Predators Algorithm (MPA)~\cite{faramarzi2020mpa}. After the
native moves of both populations, a mediator assembles hybrid vectors dimension by dimension from ranked subsets of both
populations, guided by a SARSA-style value table~\cite{sutton2018rl}. The authors compare SARSA with a ``matched random
mirror'' and report that SARSA is better on CEC2022~\cite{kumar2021cec2022} but not on their FIR filter-design problems.
Because their code and per-run results are public~\cite{flores2026code}, the claim can be re-examined precisely.

We ask four research questions:
\begin{description}
\item[RQ1] Does the SARSA learning improve MPHBS over random choices of source and rank at an identical FE budget?
\item[RQ2] Is the published random mirror matched to MPHBS, and if not, how much of the published difference does the
  mismatch explain?
\item[RQ3] Which components of MPHBS do carry its performance: the exchange step, its amount, the population memory, or
  the restructured main loop?
\item[RQ4] What general requirements follow for ablation controls of learned components?
\end{description}

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item A formal description of MPHBS's iteration, mediator and evaluation accounting (Section~\ref{sec:mphbs}), from which
  we prove that the published mirror runs fewer iterations at the same budget and, under one of the two configurations,
  differs in its memory dynamics (Section~\ref{sec:control}, Propositions~\ref{prop:iter} and~\ref{prop:memory}). Our port
  of the mirror agrees with the authors' published runs on all \res{V3-CEC2022-n} CEC2022 functions and all
  \res{V3-FIR-n} FIR cases.
\item A pre-registered study~\cite{nosek2018preregistration} with budget-identical controls that separates the four
  candidate causes, with a mechanical decision rule fixed before any run (Section~\ref{sec:design}).
\item The finding that learning brings no benefit at equal budget, that the exchange step and the memory handling carry
  the performance, and a post hoc diagnostic that links the memory effect to population diversity
  (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item A definition of budget- and state-matched controls and a protocol (Algorithm~\ref{alg:protocol}) for constructing
  and verifying them (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:related} surveys the literature. Section~\ref{sec:mphbs} formalises MPHBS and its published evidence,
and Section~\ref{sec:control} analyses the published control. Section~\ref{sec:design} presents the study design,
Section~\ref{sec:results} the results and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} their interpretation. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion}
concludes.

\section{Literature survey}
\label{sec:related}

This section surveys the five bodies of work that MPHBS and our study draw on: the two parent algorithms and their
variants, hybrid and multi-population metaheuristics, learning-based control of metaheuristics, population diversity, and
the methodology of experimental comparison. Section~\ref{sec:gap} summarises the gap that this paper addresses.

\subsection{The parent algorithms: HBA and MPA}
The Honey Badger Algorithm~\cite{hashim2022hba} alternates a ``digging'' move, which scales the distance to the best
agent by a smell-intensity term, and a ``honey'' move towards the best agent, both modulated by a decreasing density
factor. A recent survey and meta-analysis counts 52 studies proposing improved HBA variants (for example with chaotic maps,
L\'evy flights or adaptive mechanisms), 20 studies hybridising HBA with other methods and 101 applications of the original
algorithm~\cite{hassan2024hba}. The Marine Predators Algorithm~\cite{faramarzi2020mpa} switches between Brownian and L\'evy
movements in three phases of the run and adds two memory mechanisms, the marine memory and the fish aggregating device
(FAD) perturbation. A review of 102 MPA studies covers its variants and hybrids and critically discusses its convergence
behaviour and main limitations~\cite{albetar2023mpa}. Both algorithms are thus
typical of the recent generation of population-based metaheuristics, and both are frequently hybridised. MPHBS combines
the two through a learned exchange mechanism. It is not the first HBA--MPA hybrid: Al-Dhaifallah et al.\ combined the
two algorithms in series, population-division and switching schemes for a power-network planning
problem~\cite{aldhaifallah2024mpahba}. To our knowledge, however, MPHBS is the first to control their exchange with a
temporal-difference learner.

\subsection{Hybrid and multi-population metaheuristics}
Hybridising metaheuristics is a long-standing design principle~\cite{talbi2002taxonomy,blum2011hybrid}. Talbi's taxonomy
distinguishes low-level hybrids, which replace a component of one algorithm by another, from high-level hybrids, in which
self-contained algorithms cooperate, and relay hybrids, which run them in sequence, from teamwork hybrids, which run them in
parallel~\cite{talbi2002taxonomy}. Parallel and island models have long studied cooperating populations and the design of
their migration: what is exchanged, between which populations, how often and how much~\cite{alba2002parallel}. Multi-population
techniques have become widespread in nature-inspired optimisation; Ma et al.\ survey their concepts, design issues and
applications~\cite{ma2019multipop}. In this vocabulary
MPHBS is a high-level teamwork hybrid with two heterogeneous populations and a dedicated exchange phase. Its design fixes
when and how much is exchanged (one mediator phase per iteration with a fixed number of episodes) and learns only which
source and rank provide each dimension of the exchanged vectors. Unlike classical migration, which moves whole
individuals, the mediator recombines individual coordinates from both populations.

\subsection{Learning-based control of metaheuristics}
Using machine learning inside metaheuristics has become a research area of its own. Talbi classifies the synergies by the
search component that learning acts on: the target optimisation problem, and the low-level and high-level components of
the metaheuristic~\cite{talbi2021ml}. Parameter control adapts parameters during the run from feedback~\cite{karafotias2015parameter},
and adaptive operator selection treats the choice of a variation operator as a sequential decision, typically solved with
credit assignment schemes and multi-armed bandits~\cite{fialho2010bandit}. Reinforcement learning (RL) generalises these
schemes~\cite{drugan2019rl}. A recent survey of RL-assisted evolutionary algorithms organises the field by the function RL
performs: generating solutions, learning objective functions, selecting algorithms, operators or sub-populations, and
adapting parameters~\cite{song2024rlea}.

Table~\ref{tab:rl-survey} lists representative RL-controlled metaheuristics. They differ in the learner (tabular Q-learning,
SARSA, deep Q-networks), in the decision that is learned (an operator, a parameter, a component of a hyper-heuristic, or
the balance between exploration and exploitation) and in the state used. MPHBS stands out in two respects: its decisions
are made per dimension of an assembled vector, and its value table is indexed by (dimension, rank, source), so its learned
object is a recombination pattern rather than an operator or parameter. Across this literature, the usual evidence that
learning helps is a comparison with the same algorithm without the learner, either with the controlled decision fixed or
drawn at random. Recent studies make this comparison central. Mohammed et al.\ ablate the Q-learning component of an
ant colony optimiser and report faster convergence than the baseline without it~\cite{mohammed2026qlaco}; Carrasco et al.\
compare a policy-gradient orchestrator of seven metaheuristics with round-robin, random and untrained controls and find
that the policy mainly learns which members to avoid~\cite{carrasco2026orchestration}. Hanna et al.\ report that RL-based
mutation-operator selection in automated program repair produces more test-passing variants but does not patch more bugs
than uniform random selection~\cite{hanna2025apr}. Outside metaheuristics, Cheng et al.\ re-assessed a published deep-RL
macro placer against stronger baselines, including simulated annealing, on new public benchmarks~\cite{cheng2026macro}.
How closely random controls are matched to the learned variant, in evaluation budget per iteration and in state updates,
is rarely reported, however; it is the question at the centre of this paper.

\subsection{Population diversity and the exploration--exploitation balance}
Exploration and exploitation, and their measurement through population diversity, are central to understanding how
population-based algorithms behave~\cite{crepinsek2013exploration}. Loss of diversity leads to premature convergence on
suboptimal solutions, and many mechanisms have been proposed to maintain it~\cite{squillero2016diversity}. Whether a
general ``best'' balance exists is itself debated~\cite{morales2020balance}. Memory and replacement rules, such as MPA's
marine memory, are one of the levers that set this balance. Section~\ref{sec:diversity} shows that the way MPHBS updates
the MPA memory has a much larger effect on diversity, and on performance, than its learned controller.

\subsection{Experimental methodology and critical analyses}
A line of critical work deconstructs metaheuristics to determine which components actually carry their performance, and
has shown that several algorithms introduced with new metaphors reduce to known
mechanisms~\cite{sorensen2015metaphor,camacho2019iwd,camacho2022cuckoo,camacho2023exposing,aranha2022elephant}.
Methodological guidelines for comparing bio-inspired algorithms ask for appropriate benchmarks and reference algorithms,
principled validation and sound statistics~\cite{latorre2021guidelines,derrac2011tutorial}, attention to tuning
effort~\cite{eiben2011tuning}, reproducibility~\cite{lopezibanez2021reproducibility} and benchmarking
practice~\cite{bartzbeielstein2020benchmarking}. Benchmark design itself can bias conclusions: Kudela showed that test
functions with optima at the centre of the domain reward algorithms with a centre bias~\cite{kudela2022centre}, which is one
reason to complement a single suite with shifted and rotated problems such as CEC2022 and BBOB~\cite{hansen2021coco}. Our
study applies these principles to a learned control component and adds two elements that remain rare in the field: a
frozen pre-registration~\cite{nosek2018preregistration} and unit tests that prove the intended equivalences between variants.

\subsection{Research gap}
\label{sec:gap}
The surveyed work leaves three gaps that this paper addresses. First, RL-controlled metaheuristics are typically supported
by comparisons with a random or fixed control, but the conditions under which such a control isolates the effect of
learning, matching the evaluation cost per iteration and every state update, have not been made explicit. Second, MPHBS's
claim that learning drives its performance has not been re-examined, although its code and per-run results are public.
Third, the deconstruction literature has focused on metaphor-based algorithms; learned control components have received
little such scrutiny; the closest studies compare learned and random controls~\cite{hanna2025apr,carrasco2026orchestration}
but do not examine whether those controls are matched to the learned variant. We searched Scopus on 27~October~2026
(titles, abstracts and keywords) for studies citing MPHBS (none), for HBA--MPA hybrids (13 records), and for
RL-controlled metaheuristics combined with ablation, random-control or replication terms (136, 5 and 30 records). No
record re-examines MPHBS or its learned mediator, or analyses how per-iteration evaluation costs and state updates of random
controls bias ablations of learned components.

\section{MPHBS and its published evidence}
\label{sec:mphbs}

\subsection{Notation}
We minimise $f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$ over a box $\Omega=[\ell,u]^D$. MPHBS maintains an HBA population
$X^H=\{x^H_1,\dots,x^H_N\}$ and an MPA population $X^M$ of $N$ agents each, with fitness values $f^H_i$, $f^M_i$, the
best agents $p^H$, $p^M$ of each population and the global best $p^\star$ with value $f^\star$. MPA keeps a marine memory
$(\tilde X^M, \tilde f^M)$. $t=0,\dots,T-1$ is the iteration, $T$ the number of iterations, $\mathrm{clip}$ the projection
onto $\Omega$, $\odot$ the element-wise product and $U(a,b)$ a uniform random number.

\subsection{Native operators}
\paragraph{HBA} With the intensity
$I_i = r_i\,\|x^H_i - x^H_{i+1}\|^2 / (4\pi\|x^H_i - p^H\|^2 + \epsilon)$, the density factor
$\alpha_t = C\exp(-(t+1)/T)$ with $C=2$, a random sign $F\in\{-1,1\}$ and $d_{ij}=p^H_j-x^H_{ij}$, each agent proposes
\begin{equation}
\hat x_{ij} = \begin{cases}
p^H_j + F\beta I_i\,p^H_j + F r_3\alpha_t d_{ij}\,\psi_{ij} & r<0.5,\\
p^H_j + F r_7\alpha_t d_{ij} & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:hba}
\end{equation}
with $\psi_{ij}=\bigl|\cos(2\pi r_4)\bigl(1-\cos(2\pi r_5)\bigr)\bigr|$, $\beta=6$ and
$r,r_3,\dots,r_7\sim U(0,1)$, as in the authors' code. The proposal replaces $x^H_i$ if it is better.

\paragraph{MPA} With the elite $E=p^M$, $P=0.5$, the adaptive factor $\mathrm{CF}_t=(1-t/T)^{2t/T}$, Brownian steps
$R_B\sim\mathcal N(0,1)^{N\times D}$ and Lévy steps $R_L = 0.05\,\mathrm{L\acute evy}(1.5)$, an agent moves by
\begin{equation}
x^M_i \leftarrow \begin{cases}
x^M_i + P R\odot R_B\odot(E - R_B\odot x^M_i) & \text{(I)},\\
E + P\,\mathrm{CF}_t\,R_B\odot(R_B\odot E - x^M_i) & \text{(II)},\ i>\tfrac N2,\\
x^M_i + P R\odot R_L\odot(E - R_L\odot x^M_i) & \text{(II)},\ i\le\tfrac N2,\\
E + P\,\mathrm{CF}_t\,R_L\odot(R_L\odot E - x^M_i) & \text{(III)},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:mpa}
\end{equation}
where phases (I), (II), (III) are $t<T/3$, $T/3\le t<2T/3$ and $t\ge 2T/3$, and $R\sim U(0,1)^D$. After evaluation, \emph{memory saving} keeps the old position where it was better:
\begin{equation}
x^M_i \leftarrow \begin{cases}\tilde x^M_i & \tilde f^M_i < f(x^M_i),\\ x^M_i & \text{otherwise},\end{cases}
\qquad (\tilde X^M,\tilde f^M)\leftarrow (X^M, f^M).
\label{eq:memory}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{FAD stage} With probability $\mathrm{FADs}=0.2$ the population is perturbed by
$\mathrm{CF}_t\,(\ell + R\odot(u-\ell))\odot B$ with a Bernoulli$(0.2)$ mask $B$, otherwise by
$\bigl(\mathrm{FADs}(1-r)+r\bigr)(x^M_{\pi_1(i)}-x^M_{\pi_2(i)})$ for random permutations $\pi_1,\pi_2$. In MPHBS every
candidate is evaluated and accepted greedily, and the memory is set to the result.

\subsection{The SARSA mediator (Phase 2)}
\paragraph{Ranked subsets} From each population, MPHBS draws a subset of
$N_\mathrm{sub}=\min\bigl(N,\max(5,\mathrm{round}(\rho N))\bigr)$ agents, half of them the best and the rest at random,
and sorts it by fitness. The subsets form a tensor $R\in\mathbb{R}^{N_\mathrm{sub}\times D\times 2}$, where
$R_{n,d,c}$ is coordinate $d$ of the rank-$n$ agent of source $c\in\{H,M\}$.

\paragraph{Episodes} The mediator starts from $v=p^\star$, evaluates it once and runs $E=N_\mathrm{sub}N_i$ episodes. The
dimensions are split into five blocks (one if $D<10$) of size $b=\lceil D/5\rceil$, visited along a random permutation.
In episode $e$ with active block $A_e$, $K$ candidates are assembled: candidate $k$ copies $v$ and sets
$v'_d = R_{n_d,d,c_d}$ for $d\in A_e$, where $(c_d,n_d)$ is chosen by policy $\pi_k$ over the value table
$Q\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N_\mathrm{sub}\times 2}$: greedy, $\varepsilon$-greedy with
$\varepsilon_e=\varepsilon_\mathrm{end}+(\varepsilon_0-\varepsilon_\mathrm{end})e^{-(e-1)/\max(1,E/4)}$, uniform,
second-best, or $\varepsilon$-greedy at a look-ahead episode. A proxy score that costs no evaluation selects one
candidate,
\begin{equation}
s(v') = -w_\mathrm{rank}\,\overline{(n_d+1)}_{d\in A_e} + w_\mathrm{nov}\,\overline{|v'_d-v_d|}_{d\in A_e}
        - w_\mathrm{best}\,\|v'_{A_e}-p^\star_{A_e}\|_2,
\label{eq:proxy}
\end{equation}
with $w_\mathrm{rank}=1$, $w_\mathrm{nov}=0.3$, and only that candidate is evaluated.

\paragraph{Reward, acceptance and update} The reward is $r_e=\mathrm{sign}(f(v)-f(v'))$. An improving candidate is
accepted; a worse one with probability
\begin{equation}
p_e = \min\Bigl(0.25,\ 0.2\,(1-g_t)^2\bigl(0.35+0.65(1-\lambda_e)\bigr)\exp\bigl(-4\max(0,\Delta_e)\bigr)\Bigr),
\label{eq:accept}
\end{equation}
where $g_t=t/(T-1)$, $\lambda_e=(e-1)/(E-1)$ and $\Delta_e=(f(v')-f(v))/(|f(v)|+\epsilon)$. For every $d\in A_e$ the
table is updated by the SARSA rule
\begin{equation}
Q_{d,n_d,c_d} \leftarrow Q_{d,n_d,c_d} + \alpha\bigl(r_e + \gamma\,Q_{d,n'_d,c'_d} - Q_{d,n_d,c_d}\bigr),
\label{eq:sarsa}
\end{equation}
with $\alpha=0.1$, $\gamma=0.3$, where $(n'_d,c'_d)$ is the choice in $v'$ if it was accepted and $(n_d,c_d)$ otherwise.
An accepted candidate replaces the worst member of each subset that it improves. A new global best is recorded and
copied into a random subset row. At the end of Phase 2 the subsets are written back into the populations, and the bests
are synchronised. Algorithm~\ref{alg:iteration} summarises one iteration and Algorithm~\ref{alg:mediator} the
mediator.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{One iteration of MPHBS and of the study's arms. Lines marked with an arm apply only to that arm.}
\label{alg:iteration}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require populations $X^H,X^M$, memory $(\tilde X^M,\tilde f^M)$, iteration $t$, configuration (FAD placement, $K$, $N_i$)
\If{\textsc{RND-AUTH}} evaluate $X^M$ and apply memory saving \eqref{eq:memory} \Comment{$+N$ FEs}\EndIf
\State HBA move \eqref{eq:hba} and MPA move \eqref{eq:mpa}; evaluate; greedy replacement; memory saving
\eqref{eq:memory} \Comment{$2N$}
\If{FAD before Phase 2 (CEC2022)} FAD stage with greedy acceptance; set memory \Comment{$N$}\EndIf
\State $p^\star\gets$ best of $p^H,p^M$
\If{not \textsc{SKEL}} \Call{Mediator}{} (Algorithm~\ref{alg:mediator}) \Comment{$1+E$}\EndIf
\State write subsets back; synchronise $p^H,p^M,p^\star$
\If{FAD after Phase 2 (FIR)} FAD stage with greedy acceptance; set memory \Comment{$N$}\EndIf
\If{\textsc{RND-SYNC} or \textsc{SARSA-SYNC}} $(\tilde X^M,\tilde f^M)\gets(X^M,f^M)$ \Comment{0 FEs}\EndIf
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Mediator (Phase 2) of MPHBS. The random arms replace line~\ref{line:policy} by uniform draws and skip
line~\ref{line:sarsa}.}
\label{alg:mediator}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State build ranked subsets and tensor $R$; $v\gets p^\star$; $f_v\gets f(v)$ \Comment{1 FE}
\For{$e=1,\dots,E$}
  \State $A_e\gets$ next block of dimensions
  \For{$k=1,\dots,K$}
    \State $v^{(k)}\gets v$; for $d\in A_e$: $(c_d,n_d)\gets\pi_k(Q_{d,\cdot,\cdot})$; $v^{(k)}_d\gets R_{n_d,d,c_d}$
    \label{line:policy}
  \EndFor
  \State $v'\gets\arg\max_k s(v^{(k)})$ \eqref{eq:proxy}; $f'\gets f(v')$ \Comment{1 FE}
  \State $r_e\gets\mathrm{sign}(f_v-f')$; accept if $f'<f_v$, else with probability $p_e$ \eqref{eq:accept}
  \State update $Q$ on $A_e$ by \eqref{eq:sarsa} \label{line:sarsa}
  \If{accepted} $v\gets v'$, $f_v\gets f'$; replace the worst subset members that $v'$ improves\EndIf
  \If{$f'<f^\star$} $p^\star\gets v'$; copy $v'$ into a random subset row\EndIf
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Configurations and published evidence}
With $N=30$ the final configurations are B-C1 on CEC2022 ($K=7$, $N_i=3$, $\rho=0.25$, so $N_\mathrm{sub}=8$ and
$E=24$; FAD before Phase 2) and A-C1 on FIR ($K=3$, $N_i=9$, $E=72$; FAD after Phase 2), both selected by the authors
on the same benchmarks~\cite{flores2026mphbs}. The random mirror is described as identical except that source and rank
are drawn uniformly. Re-analysing the authors' 30 common-seed runs per instance with paired Wilcoxon
tests~\cite{wilcoxon1945} and Holm correction~\cite{holm1979}, SARSA is significantly better than the mirror on
\res{auth-CEC2022-vsRANDOM-sigS} of \res{auth-CEC2022-n} CEC2022 functions and worse on
\res{auth-CEC2022-vsRANDOM-sigC}, and not significantly different on any of the \res{auth-FIR-n} FIR cases. On CEC2022
F10, a bimodal composition function, SARSA escapes the dominant local basin (final error below 50) in
\res{auth-F10-SARSA-esc}/30 runs, the mirror in \res{auth-F10-RANDOM-esc}/30 and the backbone without mediator (MPHB) in
\res{auth-F10-MPHB-esc}/30.

\section{Why the published control is not matched}
\label{sec:control}

\subsection{Evaluation budget}
Runs are stopped by a total budget $B$ of FEs. The authors' runners choose $T$ as the largest number of iterations that
fits into $B$, using a linear FE model calibrated by probing.

\begin{proposition}[Iterations under a fixed budget]
\label{prop:iter}
Let a variant spend $a$ FEs on initialisation and $c$ FEs per iteration. Under budget $B$ it runs
$T=\lfloor (B-a)/c\rfloor$ iterations. MPHBS spends $c_\mathrm{S}=3N+1+E$ and the published mirror
$c_\mathrm{R}=4N+1+E$, both with $a=2N$. The mirror therefore runs
\begin{equation}
\frac{T_\mathrm{R}}{T_\mathrm{S}} \approx \frac{3N+1+E}{4N+1+E}
\label{eq:ratio}
\end{equation}
times as many iterations and episodes as MPHBS.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Every iteration of MPHBS evaluates the $N$ HBA proposals, the $N$ moved MPA agents, the $N$ FAD candidates, the
starting point of the mediator and the $E$ episode candidates. The mirror executes the same steps and, at the start of
each iteration, evaluates the $N$ MPA agents again (lines 58--66 of \texttt{MPHBS\_random\_mirror.m}). The number of
iterations follows from the stopping rule.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{cor:deficit}
With $N=30$: on CEC2022 ($E=24$, $B=200{,}000$) $c_\mathrm{S}=115$, $c_\mathrm{R}=145$, $T_\mathrm{S}=1738$ and
$T_\mathrm{R}=1378$ ($-21\%$); on FIR ($E=72$, $B=150{,}000$) $c_\mathrm{S}=163$, $c_\mathrm{R}=193$, $T_\mathrm{S}=919$
and $T_\mathrm{R}=776$ ($-16\%$). The resulting totals, \res{auth-CEC2022-RANDOM-fe} and \res{auth-FIR-RANDOM-fe} FEs for
the mirror against \res{auth-CEC2022-SARSA-fe} and \res{auth-FIR-SARSA-fe} for MPHBS, are exactly those in the authors'
result files.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Population memory}
The extra evaluation recomputes values that are already known, but it is followed by memory saving, which sets the
memory to the current population.

\begin{proposition}[When the extra block matters]
\label{prop:memory}
With a deterministic objective, the mirror's start-of-iteration block (i) never changes a position and (ii) changes the
algorithm's state only through the memory update $(\tilde X^M,\tilde f^M)\gets(X^M,f^M)$. If the FAD stage follows
Phase 2 (FIR), MPHBS performs the same update before the block, so the block has no effect other than its cost. If the
FAD stage precedes Phase 2 (CEC2022), MPHBS keeps the pre-Phase-2 memory, so the next memory saving compares moved MPA
agents with their pre-mediator positions, whereas the mirror compares them with the post-mediator population.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Between two evaluations of an MPA agent, its position changes only by Phase~2, which writes back subset members that
are equal to or better than before. Hence the re-evaluation reproduces the stored values, $\tilde f^M_i<f^M_i$ never
holds in the block's memory saving, and no position reverts. The memory is last set by the FAD stage. If that stage
follows Phase 2, the memory already equals the current population when the block runs; otherwise it predates Phase 2.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The mirror also omits the value-table bookkeeping and a closest-action projection. Once the table is not used, these
only set table indices and draw no random numbers, so they cannot affect the search. The global-best copy inside
Algorithm~\ref{alg:mediator} writes into subset rows that are overwritten when the subsets are written back, in both codes.
We confirmed Proposition~\ref{prop:memory} and the inertness of the copy with bit-identical re-runs
(Section~\ref{sec:validation}).
\end{remark}

\section{Study design}
\label{sec:design}

The design, hypotheses, run counts, statistics and decision rule were fixed in a pre-registration that was frozen and
tagged in the public repository before any campaign run (Section~\ref{sec:availability}). No analysis reported as
confirmatory deviates from it; analyses added afterwards are labelled post hoc.

\subsection{Arms}
All arms run on a Python port of the authors' code (Section~\ref{sec:validation}) with the authors' final configuration
of each benchmark (Table~\ref{tab:arms}, Fig.~\ref{fig:loop}).
\textbf{SARSA} is MPHBS unchanged.
\textbf{RND-FAIR} draws $(c_d,n_d)$ uniformly for all $K$ candidates and never reads or updates $Q$; everything else,
including the FE cost, is identical to SARSA.
\textbf{RND-SYNC} (CEC2022 only) is RND-FAIR plus the mirror's memory update at zero cost; by
Proposition~\ref{prop:memory} it would equal RND-FAIR on FIR.
\textbf{RND-AUTH} ports the published mirror, including the extra evaluations.
\textbf{SKEL} is the MPHBS loop without Phase~2.
\textbf{MPHB} is the authors' backbone without mediator, whose FAD perturbation replaces the population and is only
evaluated in the next iteration.
Secondary arms vary $N_i\in\{1,3,9\}$, use $K=1$ (no proxy screening), use greedy acceptance, or add the memory update to
SARSA (SARSA-SYNC).

\input{generated/tab_arms.tex}

\subsection{Hypotheses}
Each hypothesis is a seed-paired comparison of an anchor arm with a comparator, tested separately on each benchmark.
\begin{description}
\item[H1 (learning)] SARSA vs RND-FAIR.
\item[H2a (budget of the mirror)] RND-SYNC vs RND-AUTH on CEC2022 and RND-FAIR vs RND-AUTH on FIR; by
  Proposition~\ref{prop:memory} each pair differs only in the extra FEs.
\item[H2b (memory, CEC2022)] RND-FAIR vs RND-SYNC.
\item[H3 (mediator)] SARSA vs SKEL.
\item[H4 (loop)] SKEL vs MPHB.
\end{description}
H1--H4 are confirmatory. The F10 escape rate (H5), the number of episodes (H6), the mediator's components (H7) and the
memory update in SARSA (H8) are exploratory.

\subsection{Benchmarks, runs and seeds}
CEC2022~\cite{kumar2021cec2022} F1--F12 at $D=20$ with $B=200{,}000$ FEs, computed by the official C code; the outcome is
the final error $f-f^\star$. The FIR problems of~\cite{flores2026mphbs} design a linear filter $h\in[-1,1]^{31}$
(low-pass, high-pass, band-pass and band-stop, each in an easier and a harder variant) with $B=150{,}000$ FEs and the
objective
\begin{equation}
F(h) = W_p\,\frac{1}{|P|}\sum_{n\in P}\bigl(|H(\omega_n)|-1\bigr)^2 + W_s\,\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{n\in S}|H(\omega_n)|^2,
\label{eq:fir}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the 2048-point discrete Fourier transform of $h$, $P$ and $S$ are the pass- and stop-band grid points,
$W_p=1$ and $W_s\in\{100,120\}$. The H1 pair was run 100 times per instance and every other arm 50 times; F10 was
extended to 100 runs for the four H5 arms. Seeds are $92\,000\,000+\text{offset}+1000\cdot\text{instance}+\text{run}$ and
identical across arms, so all comparisons are seed-paired.

\subsection{Statistics and decision rule}
For instance $j$ with $n$ seed pairs $(a_s,b_s)$ of anchor and comparator outcomes we compute the paired win
probability and the median relative difference
\begin{align}
\hat w_j &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{s=1}^{n}\Bigl(\mathbb 1[a_s<b_s-\tau] + \tfrac12\,\mathbb 1\bigl[|a_s-b_s|\le\tau\bigr]\Bigr),
\label{eq:w}\\
\hat\delta_j &= \operatorname*{median}_s \frac{b_s-a_s}{|b_s|},
\label{eq:delta}
\end{align}
with $\tau=10^{-12}$, and a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. $p$-values are Holm-adjusted within each (hypothesis,
benchmark) family of instances~\cite{holm1979,derrac2011tutorial}. The domain-level effect is
$\bar W=\frac1J\sum_j\hat w_j$, with a 95\% percentile bootstrap interval~\cite{efron1979bootstrap} from 10{,}000
resamples of seed pairs within instances. $\hat\delta_j$ is descriptive and was added after the pre-registration, because
$\bar W$ measures the consistency of an effect but not its size.

\begin{definition}[Verdict]
\label{def:verdict}
Let $n_A$ and $n_C$ be the numbers of Holm-significant instances with $\hat w_j>0.5$ and $\hat w_j<0.5$, and $[L,U]$ the
interval for $\bar W$. The verdict is the first that applies: \emph{mixed} if $n_A>0$ and $n_C>0$; \emph{anchor superior}
if $n_A>0$ and $L>0.5$; \emph{comparator superior} if $n_C>0$ and $U<0.5$; \emph{practically equivalent} if $n_A=n_C=0$
and $[L,U]\subseteq[0.44,0.56]$, the negligible-effect band of Vargha and Delaney~\cite{vargha2000}; otherwise
\emph{inconclusive}.
\end{definition}

With 100 pairs per instance, $\mathrm{SE}(\bar W)\le\sqrt{0.25/(100J)}$, which gives a probability of about 0.97 (CEC2022,
$J=12$) and 0.85 (FIR, $J=8$) of an equivalence verdict when there is no effect. Escape rates are reported with Wilson
intervals~\cite{wilson1927} and compared by an exact McNemar test~\cite{mcnemar1947}.

\subsection{Execution and validation}
\label{sec:validation}
The campaign ran once on GitHub-hosted Linux runners in the pinned software environment (Python 3.11, NumPy 2.4.6),
after the full test suite had passed there. The port's MPHBS reproduces the authors' FE totals exactly and their
published run distributions~\cite{flores2026code}; the one known exception, a lower escape rate on F10, is examined in
H5. Unit tests establish that (i) the variant code with default settings is bit-identical to the validated MPHBS port;
(ii) every arm's FE total matches Table~\ref{tab:arms}; (iii) at a fixed number of iterations and on test problems,
RND-AUTH produces trajectories bit-identical to RND-FAIR under the FIR configuration and to RND-SYNC under the CEC2022
configuration, confirming Proposition~\ref{prop:memory}; and (iv) removing the global-best copy does not change any
trajectory. All \res{runs-total} runs completed with the expected FE totals, and re-running the analysis on the stored
raw results on a second machine reproduced all verdicts, effect sizes and intervals. RND-AUTH agrees with the authors'
published mirror runs on every instance (two-sided Mann--Whitney, Holm; \res{V3-CEC2022-differ} of \res{V3-CEC2022-n}
and \res{V3-FIR-differ} of \res{V3-FIR-n} instances differ).

\subsection{RQ3: what does matter (H3, H4)}
Removing the mediator altogether is very costly on FIR: SARSA beats SKEL on all eight cases

depend on learned choices of source and rank.

evaluation of the FAD stage in SKEL removes only rare deteriorations. The pre-registered verdict is correct as a statement
about consistency, but the effect is of no practical relevance; the advantage of MPHBS over MPHB comes from the mediator
(H3), not from the loop.

\subsection{Post hoc diagnostic: memory and diversity}
\label{sec:diversity}
To probe the mechanism behind H2b and H8 we analysed the stored per-iteration traces of RND-FAIR/RND-SYNC and
SARSA/SARSA-SYNC on CEC2022. The analysis was defined and committed before it was computed (after opening a single run to
learn the trace format) but after the confirmatory results were known. We measure the pooled diversity
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{div}_t = \frac1D\sum_{d=1}^{D}\frac{\operatorname{sd}\bigl(\{x_{d}: x\in X^H_t\cup X^M_t\}\bigr)}{u_d-\ell_d}
\label{eq:div}
\end{equation}
and the MPA move-acceptance rate, the share of Phase-1 MPA moves that survive memory saving \eqref{eq:memory}.

With the stale memory of MPHBS, the mean log-diversity over the run is higher on all \res{div-RND-moresig} functions for
the random pair and all \res{div-SARSA-moresig} for the SARSA pair (Holm-significant; median difference
\res{div-RND-dlog} and \res{div-SARSA-dlog} orders of magnitude), and the MPA acceptance rate is higher on all
\res{div-RND-accsig} functions (medians \res{div-RND-acc-stale}\% vs \res{div-RND-acc-fresh}\%). With the refreshed memory,
diversity falls by several orders of magnitude within the first 20--45\% of the run and reaches about $10^{-10}$ on eight of
the twelve functions (Fig.~\ref{fig:diversity}).
The size of the diversity gap, however, does not predict which functions benefit from the stale memory (Spearman
$\rho=\res{div-RND-rho}$, $p=\res{div-RND-rhop}$, $n=12$): F1 and F11 lose diversity like the others yet favour the
refresh. The mechanism is thus consistent with the direction of the effect on most functions but does not explain its
size per function.

candidates coordinate by coordinate from different agents, which preserves good building blocks when variables are
independent and destroys them when they interact, a limitation the MPHBS authors themselves anticipate for strongly
coupled variables~\cite{flores2026mphbs}. On the FIR problems, whose objective \eqref{eq:fir} couples coefficients only
weakly through a quadratic stop-band term, the mediator was uniformly beneficial (H3).

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What drives MPHBS}
Three components carry the performance of MPHBS on these benchmarks: exchanging information between the two
populations at all (decisive on FIR), the amount of exchange per iteration, and the way the MPA memory interacts with the
mediator. The SARSA controller and the restructured loop are not among them.

\paragraph{Why learning does not help}
The value table has $D\cdot N_\mathrm{sub}\cdot 2$ entries (320 on CEC2022, 496 on FIR) and receives
$b=\lceil D/5\rceil$ updates per episode, i.e.\ about $T E b/(2DN_\mathrm{sub})\approx 520$ updates per entry on CEC2022
and 930 on FIR. Data are therefore not scarce. The reward, however, is the sign of the change of a whole assembled vector,
shared equally by all $b$ dimensions of the block, and the evaluated candidate was itself chosen by the proxy score from
$K$ candidates generated by different policies. The credit that reaches an individual (dimension, rank, source) entry is
thus heavily diluted, and the most informative choices, taking coordinates from good agents, are already enforced by the
ranked subsets and the rank term of the proxy score \eqref{eq:proxy}. Under these conditions a learned policy has little
to add over uniform choices, consistent with the difficulties of credit assignment reported for adaptive operator
selection~\cite{fialho2010bandit}.

\paragraph{Why the stale memory helps}
With the stale memory, an MPA agent whose move fails returns to its pre-mediator position, which is typically far from
the incumbent, whereas with the refreshed memory it returns to the post-mediator population, whose subset rows the
mediator has filled with vectors close to the incumbent. The diagnostic in Section~\ref{sec:diversity} shows the expected
consequence: the refreshed memory makes the population collapse, which on most functions is premature convergence in the
sense of the exploration--exploitation literature~\cite{crepinsek2013exploration}. On F1 (Zakharov), a unimodal function,
faster collapse is an advantage, which fits the two exceptions only partly (F11 is a composition function). The design of
MPHBS benefits from this property, apparently by accident: it is neither described nor motivated in the original paper.

\subsection{Matched controls for learned components}
The published mirror illustrates two ways in which a control can differ from the learned variant without anybody
intending it. We make the requirements explicit.

\begin{definition}[Matched control]
\label{def:matched}
Let $\mathcal A$ be an algorithm whose decisions in a component $\mathcal C$ are taken by a learned policy, and
$\mathcal A_0$ the same algorithm with $\mathcal C$'s decisions drawn from a reference distribution. $\mathcal A_0$ is
\emph{budget-matched} if both variants spend the same number of FEs in every iteration, so that they run the same number
of iterations under the same budget, and \emph{state-matched} if, given the same decisions, both variants perform the same
updates of every state variable outside the learned component (populations, memories, archives, parameters). A control
that is budget- and state-matched isolates the effect of learning.
\end{definition}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:protocol} turns the definition into a procedure; the unit tests of our study implement its
budget and inertness checks (Section~\ref{sec:validation}).

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Constructing and verifying a matched control for a learned component}
\label{alg:protocol}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require implementation of $\mathcal A$ with learned component $\mathcal C$; reference distribution $q$
\Ensure control $\mathcal A_0$ and evidence that it is matched
\State implement $\mathcal A_0$ as a switch in the code path of $\mathcal A$ that replaces $\mathcal C$'s decisions by
  draws from $q$ and disables only the learning updates (no separate program)
\State \textbf{budget check:} measure FEs per iteration of $\mathcal A$ and $\mathcal A_0$ on a probe problem; require equality
  and identical $T$ under every budget used
\State \textbf{state check:} with $q$ set to the decisions of $\mathcal A$ (replay), require bit-identical trajectories of
  $\mathcal A$ and $\mathcal A_0$ for fixed seeds
\State \textbf{inertness checks:} for every difference not intended to matter, require bit-identical trajectories with and
  without it
\State \textbf{no-component arm:} add a variant without $\mathcal C$ to separate the value of the mechanism from that of learning
\State fix hypotheses, run counts and decision rule before running; report magnitudes alongside consistency measures
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Applied to the published mirror, the budget check would have exposed the extra $N$ FEs, and the state check, which
replays SARSA's decisions, would have exposed the memory update. A further lesson concerns effect sizes: a paired, seed-matched
design detects extremely consistent but tiny effects such as H4. Consistency measures like $\bar W$ should therefore always
be accompanied by magnitudes such as $\hat\delta$ in \eqref{eq:delta}.

\subsection{Implications for the design of hybrids}
The results suggest simplifications of MPHBS without loss: the value table can be replaced by uniform choices of source and
rank, and the number of episodes is a more important parameter than the controller. Conversely, the treatment of the MPA
memory deserves to be a deliberate design decision, because it controls population diversity and has a larger effect than
the learning. More generally, in learned hybrids the mechanism that a learned controller steers (here: dimension-wise
exchange) can be valuable even when the learning is not, and the two should be evaluated separately.

\subsection{Threats to validity}
\paragraph{Scope} We study one hybrid and the authors' final configurations. The confirmatory analysis covers two benchmark
sets at one dimension each; the exploratory BBOB extension (Section~\ref{sec:bbob}) adds 24 functions at two dimensions and
replicates H1, but it uses only three arms and 30 runs per function. The configurations were selected with SARSA on
CEC2022 and FIR, which favours SARSA and makes H1 conservative. No free lunch results~\cite{wolpert1997nfl} caution against
generalising beyond the problems studied.

\paragraph{Port and platform} Our results use a Python port; agreement with the authors' MATLAB runs is distributional
because random streams differ. The port reproduces the authors' FE totals and run distributions, and RND-AUTH reproduces
their mirror on all instances. The F10 discrepancy (H5) remains unexplained.

\paragraph{Statistics} The H1 effect is small; we claim the absence of a benefit of learning, not a large benefit of random
choice. The relative difference $\hat\delta$ was added after the pre-registration and is descriptive.

\paragraph{Exploratory parts} H5--H8 and the diversity diagnostic are exploratory and should be confirmed independently.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
The learned controller of MPHBS does not improve on random choices at an equal budget on CEC2022 or FIR. The published
evidence for it rests on a control that is handicapped by its evaluation budget and, under the CEC2022 configuration, by a
memory update that collapses population diversity. MPHBS's performance comes from exchanging information between its
populations, from the amount of exchange, and from its handling of the population memory; its restructured loop is
consistently but negligibly better than the original backbone. On the BBOB suite the learned controller again brings no
benefit, and the exchange step helps only on separable functions. More generally, ablations of learned components in
metaheuristics should be budget- and state-matched, verified mechanically, fixed in advance and reported with effect
magnitudes.
